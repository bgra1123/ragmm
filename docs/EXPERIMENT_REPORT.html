1. Executive summary

The question that drove the work. If you train the same model on the same data twice with a different random seed, how often does a real person get a different decision, who is affected most, and can a post-training "adapter" based on risk-averse optimization remove the difference? A second question came from your proposal: does the risk-averse step-length rule add anything beyond exact full-data steps?

What we found, in order of how sure we are

  1. The problem is real and concentrated. Retraining textbook SGD logistic regression on German Credit 1,000 times changed 55 of 300 held-out decisions per retrain (18%), and 284 of 300 applicants got a different answer at least once. The chance of a wrong decision was 30.4% against 24.4% for a model without training randomness, and the gap was largest for unusual applicants: housing "for free" 37.8% against 27.4% (+10.4 points), no property 35.4% against 25.6%, negative checking balance 40.0% against 31.3%. Across six datasets, the 5% most affected people carried 20% to 58% of the extra loss in bad runs. With a careful learning rate (0.01) instability falls to 0.7%-3.4% of decisions, so the large effect belongs to aggressive step sizes and to models trained by SGD-type methods.
  2. RA-GMM as a trainer is not the fix. It beats textbook momentum (about half the flips, 54-77% lower worst-case loss), but plain SGD with a step size tuned to the same average does as well, and SGD with a tuned step and tail averaging is far better (6.6 flips against 50.8). The portfolio experiments gave the same pattern, and the paper's quadratic formulation (problem 32) was worse than textbook SGD there because minibatch noise grows with the weights.
  3. A short, exact polish after training is the fix that works for convex models. Continuing from the trained weights with 20 full-batch steps cut two-retrain disagreement from 15.5% to 0.1% (unstable rows 35.3% to 0.3%) and lowered the wrong-decision rate from 27.3% to 25.4%. The groups hit hardest gained most (unemployed 41.8% to 36.3%, housing for free 35.1% to 30.1%). For trees and neural networks no gradient polish helps, because the seed changes the model's structure; averaging 10 seeds cuts disagreement by about two thirds (XGBoost 6.5% to 2.1%, MLP 14.4% to 5.1%). But 10 times more trees gives the same stability at the same cost, so for trees the adapter is a convenience, not a new capability.
  4. Against "one more epoch of training" the adapter wins in most settings. With an Adam start it wins from the first epoch on all four datasets. With an SGD start it wins from about 2 to 5 epochs, but on a large dataset (COMPAS) average-and-continue is better at 1 to 2 epochs. At 20 epochs the adapter wins everywhere (0.0 to 0.1% against 0.5 to 3.8%).
  5. The risk adjustment itself is not yet shown to add value. Your first-order entropic step matches an ordinary exact line search on stability. Against a plain line search shrunk to the same average step it hurts slightly fewer rows (median difference 0.002 in the 95th-percentile per-row loss increase, small beside harm levels of 0.03 to 1.1) but gives up some seed stability, has no effect on the worst group's wrong-decision rate, and causes no fewer negative flips (rows that were right and become wrong). The gain over simply training longer comes from using exact full-data gradients.
  6. Measurement is a product on its own. The tail report, the per-group "chance of a wrong decision" statements and stability_check work for any model, and nothing else in the repository delivers that.

What is open

  • Experiment C (large datasets where only a few passes are affordable) is the regime where a careful step rule could still matter; it has not been run.
  • Neural networks beyond a small MLP, and LLMs: the harness is built and tested on a stand-in model; real runs need model access this environment does not have.
  • Data-resampling instability (new month, new sample) with an exact solver, the "Gate 0b" test, was recommended but not run.

Recommended positioning

"Measure how much your model's decisions depend on the seed, overall and per group, and remove the part that can be removed." Describe the risk-averse step as a self-tuning short-step rule with a harm-control knob, not as better worst-case behaviour. Say plainly that more trees or a longer training run is an equally good fix for tree models.

2. Background, definitions and common setup

2.1 The method and the proposal

GMM and RA-GMM. The generalized momentum method updates y_k = x_k + γ d_k, d_{k+1} = β d_k − α ∇f(y_k), x_{k+1} = x_k + d_{k+1}. Gradient descent (β = γ = 0), the heavy-ball method (γ = 0) and Nesterov (γ = β) are special cases. With noisy gradients the paper bounds the entropic value at risk (EV@R) of the final error and picks (α, β, γ) to make that bound small subject to a rate constraint (problem 51 for strongly convex objectives; problem 32 and Theorem 1 for quadratics). The EV@R is EV@R_{1−ζ}[X] = inf over t > 0 of (1/t) log(E[e^{tX}]/ζ).

Your line-search proposal. Given a model w with full gradient g = ∇F(w), choose α* = argmin over α of (1/θ) log( (1/n) Σ_i exp(−θ α gᵀ∇f_i(w)) ), then update w ← w − α* g. This is the entropic risk of how a step changes each training row's loss. As typed with a leading minus the problem is unbounded, so the repository minimizes the entropic risk of the loss change. In the code this is step="entropic". A variant added here, step="entropic_exact", uses the exact per-row loss change instead of the first-order one.

Part A. Establishing the problem and testing the paper's method

E1 RA-GMM against baselines on German Credit (model multiplicity)

Status: completed; research/model_multiplicity

Question. Does the paper's RA-GMM (problem 51), with no tuning runs, reduce how often retrained credit models flip decisions, compared with textbook settings and with settings tuned for the actual budget?

What we did. Trained logistic regression on 700 German Credit applicants and tested on 300. 200 seeds per method, K in {500, 2000}, λ in {0.003, 0.01, 0.03}. Methods: textbook SGD, Nesterov and heavy-ball; SGD and Nesterov tuned for the mean (240 and 1,440 tuning runs); SGD with tail averaging (tuned); RA-GMM at ε = 0.25 and 0.05 (0 tuning runs).

Outcome.

  • Textbook Nesterov changed 99.8 of 300 decisions per retrain on average (worst 5%: 125); textbook SGD 71.3; 98-100% of applicants had a seed-dependent decision.
  • RA-GMM (ε = 0.25) changed 50.8 (worst 5%: 65), about half of textbook momentum (paired difference vs Nesterov −49.0, 95% CI −51.2 to −46.8), and its 95th-percentile suboptimality was 0.12 against 2.1.
  • Tuned baselines were far better: SGD with tail averaging changed 6.6 (worst 5%: 10), expected cost 0.550 against 0.659 for RA-GMM.
  • Diagnosis: at condition number about 99 the rate constraint of problem 51 is vacuous (ε = 0.25 does not bind), so the minimizer slides to the smallest step on the grid; the bound is asymptotic while training is finite; the parametrization ties momentum to step size; and RA-GMM used zero tuning runs against 240-1,440 for the baselines.
  • Library finding: version 0.1.0 chose α = 1e-6 (the model barely trained) because of a rate-benchmark and a β-formula error; both were fixed in 0.2.0 and the library is tested against the paper's formulas.

Verdict. H1 and H2 supported (RA-GMM beats textbook momentum); H3 answered against RA-GMM (it is clearly worse than tuned SGD with averaging). The honest claim: it halves arbitrary decisions with no tuning, but is not competitive with a tuned step plus tail averaging.

E2 Portfolio rebalancing (P1-P5 and a bound diagnostic)

Status: completed; research/portfolio

Question. Does the same pattern hold on a quadratic portfolio problem, and does the paper's quadratic formulation (problem 32) help?

What we did. A monthly rebalancing backtest on 10 US stocks (328 rebalances, 1991-2018, 200 solver seeds, K = 200, minibatch of 16 days), unconstrained (P1) and long-only (P2); a diagnostic of whether the EV@R bound can choose settings; tuning for the worst case rather than the mean (P3); and the paper's quadratic problem 32 with an exact finite-horizon EV@R extension (P5). Not investment advice: the stocks were chosen in 2018 (survivorship bias).

Outcome.

  • P1: the exact solver returns 23.3% a year (Sharpe 1.02, 303x terminal wealth). Textbook SGD and momentum trade 192-220% of wealth per rebalance purely because of the seed and ruin the account in 17-34% of seeds; tuned SGD trades 18-20% (the exact strategy trades 29%) and ends at 236-386x. RA-GMM (ε = 0.25) cut ruin from 34% to 8.5% against textbook Nesterov with zero tuning but was far worse than tuned solvers (168% phantom trades against 18%).
  • P2 (long-only): no ruin is possible; the seed still moves terminal wealth from 56x to 1,060x with textbook settings, tail-averaged SGD keeps it at 236-320x around the exact 279x.
  • Diagnostic: the finite-horizon bound is about 4.6 times larger than what tuned SGD actually achieves in its worst 5%, and on 96% of dates it exceeds the starting error, so minimizing it freezes the portfolio.
  • P3: tuning for the worst case (EV@R) rather than the average cut the 95th-percentile phantom trades from 32.4% to 29.2% and the worst-5% solver error by 17%, a modest gain.
  • P5: the paper's problem 32 at ε = 0.25 was worse than textbook Nesterov and SGD (95th-percentile error 5.28, ruin 37%, median terminal wealth 3.7x against 303x exact). My exact-EV@R extension predicted the simulated tail at 40 of 40 dates (median ratio 1.003) but did not beat tuned SGD. With the real minibatch noise the paper's bound was below the empirical EV@R; with additive Gaussian noise the prediction matched, which locates the cause: the minibatch gradient noise grows with the weights.

Verdict. The theory is right on its own model and its worst-case reporting holds up; automatic tuning by the closed-form recipes does not transfer. This is why the product moved from "tune with RA-GMM" to "measure and polish".

E3 Gate 0: does instability exist when training is careful?

Status: completed; research/gate0 (pre-registered gate)

Question. Is seed instability at least 5% of held-out decisions on at least 3 of 6 datasets, before building a product on it?

What we did. L2 logistic regression, 200 seeds, six datasets, four training settings: SGD with step 1/L, Nesterov at 1/L, SGD at learning rate 0.01, and tuned SGD with tail averaging.

Outcome.

  • With the theory's step size (a learning rate of 0.66-1.9 on standardized features) flips against the exact model were 18.4% (German), 21.5% (COMPAS), 13.6% (Adult), 5.6% (Bank), 9.8% (Pima), 5.2% (Heart); two retrains disagreed on 25.1%, 29.2%, 18.8%, 8.1%, 14.0%, 7.6%.
  • With learning rate 0.01 the flips were 0.7-3.4% and disagreement 0.9-2.9%; tuned SGD with averaging 0.4-1.9%. Retrain AUC under the aggressive step was up to 0.18 below the exact model, so stability and accuracy move together.
  • Two limits: logistic regression is usually fitted with a deterministic solver with no seed instability, so the issue concerns SGD/Adam-trained models; and only optimizer randomness was varied, not data resampling.

Verdict. Gate passed as written (6 of 6 datasets unstable at the aggressive step), with a caveat that changed the pitch: the effect is large for aggressive step sizes and 1-3% for a careful default. The "98% of applicants depend on luck" figure applies only to the aggressive regime. Data-resampling instability (Gate 0b) was recommended and not run.

E4 Who pays for the worst retrain (tail analysis)

Status: completed; research/tail_analysis

Question. Are the people hurt in the worst 5% of retrains typical or unusual, and does RA-GMM change the distribution of who is hurt?

What we did. Six datasets, 1,000 retrains per method on identical minibatch sequences. Tail models are the 50 retrains with the highest training objective. For each training row, the extra loss in the tail models; profile of the top 5% of people; leverage (how poorly the data pins down the direction a row's prediction depends on) and rarity of categories; RA-GMM against textbook SGD and against SGD at the same average objective.

Outcome.

  • The top 5% of people carry 58% (Adult), 45% (Bank), 32% (COMPAS), 29% (Pima), 25% (Heart) and 20% (German) of the tail's extra loss. They are the unusual ones: Adult, born outside the US (89% against 6%); COMPAS, juvenile records and younger; Pima, extreme insulin and skin thickness; German, unemployed (29% against 4%).
  • Leverage predicts a person's tail loss better than difficulty for the exact model (Spearman 0.51-0.77) on 5 of 6 datasets; the people are often not misclassified by the exact model (Adult 27%, COMPAS 32%, the same as everyone).
  • RA-GMM cut the worst-case extra loss by 54-77% against textbook SGD, but against SGD at the same average objective the tail was equal within −3% to +10% (ratio 0.97-1.10) and slightly heavier on 4 of 6 datasets: a one-parameter step-size change reproduces it. The people hurt differ between methods (Jaccard 0.00-0.37, Adult 0.75) but the kind of people does not.
  • With tuned SGD plus tail averaging the tail is 90-550 times smaller and the leverage effect almost disappears.

Verdict. H1 (concentration) supported on 4 of 6 datasets; H2 (leverage) supported; H3 (same individuals across methods) not supported; H4 first part supported (0.23-0.46x), second part not. A concrete, checkable insight: the worst retrains are paid for by unusual customers. Race and sex were removed but the effect lands on correlated groups; this is an observation about retraining variability, not a measure of bias in the exact model.

Part B. Making it better: the polish adapter and its alternatives

E5 Fixed learning rate, the polish idea, and the Gaussian-noise check

Status: completed; research/polish (E1 and E2, plus noise_check)

Question. At a given learning rate, can RA-GMM improve the tail? And can a short low-noise phase after any trainer ("polish"), with parameters chosen from an exact EV@R model, cut the tail? Is the Gaussian-noise assumption in that model harmful?

What we did. E1: for step sizes {1, 0.3, 0.1, 0.03}/L, momentum chosen by the paper's bound and by the exact finite-horizon EV@R, on six datasets. E2: stage 1 = 2,000 SGD steps or five iterations of consensus ADMM, then 1,000 further steps by continue, decay, average, RA-GMM (51) or the exact-EV@R polish. Noise check: 48 settings, 10,000 simulated paths, real minibatch noise against Gaussian noise with the same covariance.

Outcome.

  • E1: the exact-EV@R selection chose β = γ = 0 (plain SGD) in all 24 cells. The paper's choice adds momentum (β 0.76-0.97) and has a worse tail than SGD at the same step (2.6-62x). Step size is the dominant lever: no momentum at 1/L reaches SGD at 0.03/L.
  • E2: the polish lowered the tail in all 8 dataset-stage combinations, by 29-64x for SGD stage 1 and 6.5-20x for early-stopped ADMM; decisions changed fell by 74-91% (German 55 to 14; COMPAS 399 to 53). The paper's problem 51 was a poor polisher (6-30x worse).
  • The polish was within 10% of the best simple finisher in 6 of 8 cells; averaging or a small step was as good in most cells and sometimes better.
  • Noise check: real minibatch noise gave an EV@R 0.93-1.35 times the Gaussian value (median 1.06); the Gaussian formula underestimates by up to 35% only for large effective steps.

Verdict. Hypotheses H1 and H2 (RA-GMM beats SGD / Nesterov at a given step) false; H6 (polish lowers the tail) and H8 supported. "RA-GMM as a finishing step" is supported, "RA-GMM as a better optimizer at a fixed learning rate" is not: at a fixed step the best momentum is zero. The Gaussian assumption costs little in the regime the polisher uses.

E6 Adam and other optimizers, with the polish

Status: completed; research/optimizers (pre-registered, no deviations)

Question. Is instability specific to SGD, and does the polish work after Adam, RMSprop, momentum and Adagrad?

What we did. 18 optimizer configurations on four datasets (72 cells), 2,000 stage-1 steps, 1,000 polish steps, 300 seeds. Vectorized optimizers match torch.optim to 3e-16.

Outcome.

  • Learning rate matters more than the optimizer. At lr 0.1, Adam, RMSprop and momentum SGD have 9-17 times the worst-case loss of plain SGD at the same lr. Run-to-run disagreement ranged 0.018-0.246 (German); Adam at lr 0.1 was as unstable as momentum SGD.
  • PyTorch's default Adam lr (0.001) is stable but biased (bias 0.14-0.24).
  • The exact-EV@R polish lowered the tail in 72 of 72 cells (median 6.7x, range 1.9-57x) and never made a cell worse; decay worsened 21 cells, averaging 30, and RA-GMM (51) 50.
  • After the polish, retrain disagreement was 0.019-0.049 (German) regardless of the stage-1 optimizer: the polish washes out the choice of optimizer.

Verdict. Instability is a feature of every optimizer here and Adam's defaults do not protect against it. "Train with whatever you use, then polish" is justified for logistic models; fixed finishers can hurt, the data-driven polish did not.

E7 How to choose the polish parameters: Gaussian quadratic vs real noise vs simulation

Status: completed; research/selection

Question. Does dropping the Gaussian assumption (a linearized real-noise simulation, or direct simulation of real polish runs) pick better polish parameters than the closed-form Gaussian quadratic formula?

What we did. Textbook SGD stage 1, a 500-step polish, 42 candidates, 12 pilot runs per selector call, five independent pilot sets per dataset, evaluation on 300 fresh seeds. Regret is the evaluation EV@R of the chosen candidate over the best of the 42.

Outcome.

  • Median regret was at most 1.15 for the quadratic and direct selectors on all four datasets. The worst single selection was 3.2x (COMPAS, one of five pilot sets, for both).
  • The three selectors tie on average (quadratic 1.14, linearized 1.16) and fail on the same pilot sets: the failure is in the estimates from 12 pilots, not the noise model.
  • The direct selector was not 20 times slower (4.5-11.5 s against 4.8-44.7 s); costs depend on implementation.

Verdict. Dropping the Gaussian assumption did not improve the choice. Default: the quadratic approximation with estimated curvature, noise covariance and starting error, with more pilots. Direct optimization is right only where linearization is doubtful.

E8 Your risk-averse line search at equal compute

Status: completed; research/linesearch (one deviation recorded before results)

Question. How does the entropic line search α* = argmin_α (1/θ) log mean_i exp(−θ α gᵀ∇f_i) compare with exact line search and with the stochastic polisher at the same number of epochs, and what does the risk term protect?

What we did. Stage 1 = 2,000 textbook SGD steps; polish budgets of 1, 5 and 20 epochs on German, COMPAS, Pima and Heart, 200 seeds. Arms: stochastic adapter, SGD average, exact line search, R1 (first-order) at θ = 1 and 5, R3 (exact change) at θ = 5, and R4 (tilted loss). The step range was widened from 2/L to 1/μ before any performance result because the search sat on the cap.

Outcome.

  • At 20 epochs the exact line search and R1 (θ = 1) leave the worst-5% gap at most 5e-5, against 0.0007-0.013 for the stochastic adapter. At 5 epochs they win by 3-50x on German, Pima and Heart, but not on COMPAS (adapter 0.0023 against 0.0108). At 1 epoch the stochastic polishers win on German and COMPAS.
  • The small-θ closed form α ≈ ‖g‖²/(θ Var a_i) underestimates the exact minimizer by 25-35% because the a_i are skewed (skewness 2.1-2.5).
  • R1 at θ = 1 takes steps like an exact line search (2-7 times 1/L); larger θ makes it conservative. At θ = 5 (R1, R3) the 95th-percentile per-row loss increase fell 80-90% (German +0.07 against +0.69), but the objective tail at one epoch was 2.5-5x worse because the steps are 4-6 times smaller.
  • R4 (risk of the new loss with a plain-gradient direction) stalled and was the worst variant.

Verdict. A sound, cheap line search that acts as a data-driven damping of the step. It exposed the baseline the stochastic adapter should have faced: deterministic full-batch descent on the head. Where full passes are affordable, solve the head deterministically; the stochastic adapter is for very large n or one pass per update.

E9 Where the noise lands: groups and concrete statements

Status: completed; research/groups (pre-registered, including the rule that picked the showcase group)

Question. Is training noise concentrated on certain applicant groups, can we state it as a concrete chance of a wrong decision per type of applicant, and does the adapter help those groups?

What we did. German Credit, five train/test splits (300 held-out applicants each, 1,500 cases), 100 retrains per split, ten pre-specified groups (such as unemployed, housing "for free", no property, negative checking balance, under 25, not a foreign worker, women, large and long loans, divorced or separated men). Textbook SGD and Adam, each polished by the stochastic adapter (5 epochs) or by a deterministic line search. Sex, age and foreign-worker status were never model inputs.

Outcome.

  • Textbook SGD flips 19-29% of decisions for the groups against 12.5% for applicants in none of them (ratios 1.5-2.3).
  • Chance of a wrong decision, standard training against the exact model (no randomness) and against the adapter: all applicants 30.4%, 24.4%, 25.8%; housing for free 37.8%, 27.4%, 31.5%; no property 35.4%, 25.6%, 29.2%; negative checking balance 40.0%, 31.3%, 34.1%; unemployed 42.9%, 37.3%, 40.1%.
  • The part due to training randomness fell by 50-100% in every group with the adapter (for example +10.4 to +4.1 points for housing for free); the deterministic line search flipped fewer decisions still (G3 4.9%).
  • At the cost cutoff (approve at 83%), a defaulter was approved 26.6% of the time with standard training, 9.1% without randomness and 13.1% with the adapter.
  • What the adapter does not remove: the exact model itself refuses 24.7% of repaying "housing for free" applicants against 4.3% outside the groups.

Verdict. H1 supported 6 of 6; H2, H3 supported; the adapter reduces noise in all ten groups. This is the source of the plain-language statements used in the blog and videos. Limits: one dataset, groups of 36-466 cases, intervals reflect training randomness only.

E10 Credit outcomes with and without a polish

Status: completed; research/report (credit_with_adapter, pre-registered addendum)

Question. For seven ways of training a credit model, how do wrongly denied, wrongly approved, coin-flip applicants and expected cost change if a polish step is added?

What we did. German Credit, 300 held-out applicants (210 repaid, 90 defaulted), 300 seeds. Trainers: textbook SGD, RA-GMM from scratch, momentum 0.9, Adam (lr 0.001), five-iteration ADMM, careful SGD. Each polished by the adapter, the exact line search or R1 (θ = 5), at 5 epochs (20 as sensitivity).

Outcome.

  • Textbook SGD at the 50% rule: decisions changed against the exact model 55.8, with the adapter 23.2, with line search 8.2; coin-flip applicants 177, 71, 21; good applicants wrongly denied 19.6%, 13.9%, 13.0% (exact 12.9%). Errors caused by the training run fell 62% with the adapter.
  • At the 83% rule, textbook SGD expected cost fell from 0.751 to 0.595 (adapter) and 0.592 (line search); the exact model is 0.553.
  • For trainers that were already good (careful SGD, Adam at its default) the adapter changed little. The line search at 5 epochs was at least as close to exact as the adapter for all trainers (7 of 7).
  • Caution: early-stopped ADMM varies only with its random partition (1 coin-flip applicant); the stochastic adapter raised that to 38; the deterministic polish left it at 0.
  • Polishing makes decisions reproducible, not better than the exact model: the remaining 27 wrongly denied and 53 wrongly approved belong to the model. Lower cost is not the same as closer to exact: Adam at its default and ADMM were biased toward refusing, which paid under the 5:1 cost.

Verdict. The adapter moves outcomes toward the exact model (direction 7 of 7; the 50% cut holds only for the worst trainers) and the deterministic polish does better at equal compute. A stochastic polish should not be applied to a deterministic trainer.

Part C. Beyond logistic regression: trees and neural networks

E11 XGBoost seed instability and which levers work

Status: completed; research/trees (one deviation: the head refit used L-BFGS instead of Newton)

Question. Does XGBoost with subsampling have seed instability, and which fix works: a gradient-based head refit, averaging, a slower learning rate, or no subsampling?

What we did. XGBoost 3.4.1, 200 seeds (the seed controls row and column subsampling), German, COMPAS, Pima, Heart, threshold 0.5.

Outcome.

  • Default disagreement 5.5% (German), 2.3% (COMPAS), 4.0% (Pima), 1.6% (Heart). No subsampling gives exactly 0 on all four.
  • Averaging 20 seeds: 0.8%, 0.8%, 0.9%, 0.6% (65-86% reduction); averaging 5 seeds 2.2%, 1.1%, 1.4%, 0.6%. A slower learning rate with 1,000 trees cut 45-57%. A last-layer refit on leaf indicators changed disagreement by −11% to +10% and raised it on German and Pima.
  • Held-out AUC of default XGBoost was within about 0.02 of exact logistic regression.

Verdict. The randomness is in the structure of the trees, so refitting the head cannot undo it. The effective levers are seed averaging, a smaller learning rate with more trees, or turning subsampling off.

E12 XGBoost, LightGBM, random forest, HistGradientBoosting and MLP, with adapters and calibration

Status: completed; research/sota and research/sota2 (sota2 pre-registered; one deviation: averaged ensembles are 200 random 10-seed subsets)

Question. How stable are the models used in practice, and what do head refit, seed averaging and calibration change, overall, by group and at the cost-based cutoff?

What we did. German Credit, three stratified splits (900 held-out applicants), 100 retrains per model and split. Adapters: head refit (leaf indicators or penultimate activations), averaging of 10 seeds, one-dimensional logistic calibration on out-of-fold predictions, and combinations; for logistic regression the polish or line search.

Outcome.

  • Disagreement without adapter: SGD logistic 24.9%, MLP 14.8%, LightGBM 6.3%, XGBoost 5.6%, random forest 4.1%, HistGradientBoosting 0 (deterministic).
  • Averaging 10 seeds cut disagreement 68-72% (XGBoost 5.6% to 1.6%, LightGBM 6.3% to 2.0%, random forest 4.1% to 1.2%, MLP 14.8% to 4.7%).
  • Head refit: no gain for the MLP on German (14.8% to 14.8%), and it hurt trees (random forest 4.1% to 9.1%). On COMPAS it halved the MLP's disagreement (4.7% to 2.3%), so it is useful only where the head is the source of variation.
  • Calibration lowered the expected cost at the 5/6 cutoff for every model (XGBoost 0.562 to 0.530, LightGBM 0.634 to 0.552, random forest 0.530 to 0.500, MLP 0.780 to 0.578). Calibrated and averaged random forest cost 0.487 against 0.497 for exact logistic regression.
  • Where noise lands: SGD logistic, MLP, random forest and LightGBM flip 1.3 times or more as often in the unusual-profile groups; XGBoost spreads noise evenly (only 2 of 6 groups).
  • The tail above the mean in cost fell 47-56% with averaging; the average cost of the worst 5% of retrains barely moved because the mean dominates.

Verdict. H3 (averaging ≥ 50%) supported for every randomized model; H2 (head refit) not supported on German; H5 (calibration lowers cost) supported. For trees and networks the answer is averaging plus calibration, not a gradient polish.

E13 Function-space polish for XGBoost (boosting as gradient descent)

Status: completed; research/boost (pre-registered, no deviations)

Question. Because boosting is gradient descent in function space, does continuing with deterministic full-data trees, with fixed, line-search or risk-averse steps, remove the seed variation?

What we did. German Credit, three splits, 100 seeds per variant. Baseline of 300 noisy rounds; 200 noisy plus 100 deterministic rounds; 300 plus 100; 100 deterministic rounds with line-search steps and with entropic steps (θ = 5); and the average of 10 baseline seeds.

Outcome.

  • Disagreement: baseline 5.6%; deterministic continuation 5.7% (budget matched) and 5.3% (extra rounds); line-search steps 10.3%; risk-averse steps 7.7%; averaging 10 seeds 1.6%.
  • The adaptive steps over-fit: AUC fell from 0.80 to 0.77-0.78 and held-out log-loss rose from 0.53 to 0.79-0.91, because a criterion measured on training rows rewards fitting them when the base learner is flexible.

Verdict. Hypotheses H1-H3 not supported. The instability is in the structure of the early subsampled trees, not an optimization error that more gradient steps repair; boosting has no unique optimum to converge to. A capped step (α ≤ η) was not tested.

E14 Benchmark of the shipped package (StabilityAdapter, version 0.4.0)

Status: completed; research/product (pre-registered; no deviations)

Question. Do the package's default adapters reduce seed dependence overall and for the pre-registered groups, at what cost, and against equal-compute alternatives?

What we did. German Credit, five stratified splits (300 held-out applicants each), 30 retrains per configuration, default settings. SGD logistic (5 epochs, polish of 20 full-batch epochs with line search, and the entropic step), XGBoost, LightGBM, random forest and MLP (each averaged over 10 seeds). Equal-compute comparisons: SGD trained 25 times longer; 10 times the trees.

Outcome.

  • Two retrains disagree, before to after: SGD logistic 15.5% to 0.1%, XGBoost 6.5% to 2.1%, LightGBM 7.2% to 2.2%, random forest 4.2% to 1.5%, MLP 14.4% to 5.1%. Unstable rows for SGD logistic 35.3% to 0.3%; changed decisions per retrain 32.2 to 0.3.

  • SGD groups, wrong decision before to after: unemployed 41.8% to 36.3%, housing for free 35.1% to 30.1%, no property 32.0% to 27.9%, checking account below 0 DM 37.0% to 32.3%, unskilled non-resident (36 cases) 28.8% to 16.7%. Boosted trees showed small group gains.

  • Equal compute: 10 times the trees matched averaging (XGBoost 2.0% against 2.1%; LightGBM 2.1% against 2.2%; random forest 1.4% against 1.5%); SGD trained 25 times longer reached 0.3% against 0.1% for the polish.

E15 Adapter against one more epoch of the same training

Status: completed; research/risk_effect (pre-registered, section 2)

Question. For the same extra compute, is the risk-adjusted adapter better than training one more epoch?

What we did. The same 200 stage-1 models per dataset (SGD at 1/L, or Adam at 0.01) on German, COMPAS, Pima and Heart, each given 1, 2, 5 or 20 extra epochs of row-gradients. Arms: continue (C1), continue with decayed step (C2), continue and average the iterates (C3), your first-order entropic step (θ = 1), the exact-change variant (θ = 5), and the exact line search. One full-batch step counts as one epoch. Deviations: four datasets instead of five (Adult subsample not run); groups only on German.

Outcome.

  • Plain continuation does nothing for stability (for example 25.3% on German after one SGD epoch, the stage-1 level). Average-and-continue is the strongest baseline.
  • Adam start: the risk-averse first-order step beat the best continuation at 1 and 2 epochs on all four datasets (German 3.7% against 8.4% at one epoch; COMPAS 1.9% against 3.4%), and reached 0.0% at 20 epochs.
  • SGD start: at 1 epoch it won on only 1 of 4 datasets and was clearly worse on COMPAS (10.7% against 3.4%); at 2 epochs 3 of 4; from 5 epochs it won everywhere except a tie on COMPAS; at 20 epochs 0.0-0.1% against 0.5-3.8%.
  • Wrong-decision rate overall stayed within about 1 point of the baseline in nearly every cell. Worst group on German: 39.0% (risk step) against 39.3% (average-and-continue) after one SGD epoch.
  • Cost in this NumPy implementation: continuing or averaging all 200 seeds took under 0.5 s on German, the full-batch steps with the original grid search 0.6-22 s (later fixed, E18).

Figure 2. Two retrains disagree against extra epochs: average-and-continue against the risk-averse step. The dashed line is the model before any update.

Verdict. Pre-registered rule met when the start is Adam, not met at one epoch when the start is SGD. The adapter beats "one more epoch" clearly in most settings, with an exception for large data at 1-2 epochs.

E16 Step-matched controls and the theta sweep

Status: completed; research/risk_effect (plan addendum written before running)

Question. Is the risk-averse step better than simply taking a smaller step? Does the effect grow with θ?

What we did. Same 200 starting models, same full gradient at every step, 1, 2 and 5 steps, four datasets, SGD and Adam starts. Arms: exact line search (L); R1 and R3 at θ in {0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10}; control M1 (the line search with every step multiplied by one constant so its mean step equals R's); control M2 (R's mean step of each iteration replayed as one fixed schedule for all seeds). Outcome: 95th-percentile per-row loss increase on held-out rows. Paired bootstrap over seeds (1,000 resamples). The step search used a vectorized safeguarded Newton solver that agrees with the grid search to 5e-7.

Outcome.

  • Dose-response holds: larger θ gives shorter steps (R1 at θ = 5 takes 0.4-1.8 times 1/L against 2-6 times for the line search) and less harm; the mean harm falls monotonically on all four datasets in 10 of 12 settings.
  • Against the exact line search the risk step hurts far fewer rows (German, SGD, 2 steps, R3 at θ = 5: −0.43), but it also takes 3-7 times shorter steps.
  • Over the 160 settings with 2 or 5 steps, the risk step has significantly lower harm than M1 in 99 (median difference −0.002) and than M2 in 83, significantly worse than M2 in 20 (median −0.002).
  • In 78 of the 99 settings where it beats M1 on harm, it has significantly more seed disagreement (typically +0.3 to +4 points); only 9 settings are better on harm and no worse on disagreement.
  • Worst-group wrong-decision rate on German: risk step minus M1 between −0.3 and +0.6 points in every setting.

Verdict. Credit rule (lower harm than L and M1 on at least 3 of 4 datasets for some θ): met from two steps on, not at one step; group part not met; the effect size is small. Most of the harm reduction is step length. The risk-averse step is a way to take shorter steps that adapt a little to each seed, paid for in seed stability.

E17 Negative-flip test (does the risk step avoid regressions?)

Status: completed; research/risk_effect (plan addendum before running)

Question. When a model is updated, does the risk-averse step cause fewer negative flips (rows decided correctly before and wrongly after) than matched controls and continued training?

What we did. The same arms as E16 plus the continuation arms, at 1, 2 and 5 epochs, 200 models, four datasets. Counts of negative and positive flips per held-out row; German worst-group negative-flip rate. Credit rule fixed in advance.

Outcome.

  • SGD start, 1 epoch, four-dataset average: continued training flips 9.65% of rows from correct to wrong (net +0.02 points); average-and-continue 5.59% (net +2.35); exact line search 5.50% (+2.33); risk step R1 (θ = 5) 0.77% (+0.65); line search with the same mean step (M1) 0.77% (+0.65).
  • Over 240 cells the risk step has significantly fewer negative flips than the line search in 179 and than the best continuation in 190 (median about 0.6 points of rows), but against M1 only 85 fewer, 46 more and 109 not significant (median −0.004 points), and against M2 104 fewer and 16 more (median −0.034).
  • The reduction mostly comes from the model moving less: positive flips fall with the negative ones. Negative flips per point of net gain: line search 2.4, risk step 1.2, M1 1.2 at one epoch; at five epochs 1.9, 1.5 and 1.6.
  • German worst-group negative-flip rate, SGD, five steps: risk step 5.84% against 8.12% for M1; one dataset only.

Verdict. Credit rule not met (best settings pass on 2 of 4 datasets; none at one step). Short exact steps avoid regressions; the risk-averse rule is one way to choose them and is no better than a plain line search of the same size.

E18 Streaming step search (computing the sums in batches) and cost

Status: completed; package risk_averse_tuning.adapter, research/risk_effect/cost_benchmark.py

Question. The step rule needs only sums over rows once the gradient is known. Can it be computed batch by batch with the same answer, and what does that do to cost?

What we did. Implemented polish_logistic_streaming with two modes: accumulate log-sum-exp sums on a 120-point grid (memory O(grid)), or cache three length-n vectors and solve the convex 1-D problem by safeguarded Newton (memory O(n)). Two passes per step (full gradient; per-row quantities along it). Tested against the in-memory version for three step rules and θ = 1 and 5, several batch sizes. Timed on German, COMPAS, Adult and a 200,000-row synthetic set (timing only).

Outcome.

  • Correctness: the streaming result matches the in-memory polish to about 1e-8 in the weights with Newton and 1e-3 with the grid, and does not depend on the batch size (below 1e-9). 127 tests pass in total.
  • 20 extra epochs, seconds for one model: German SGD-continue 0.019, entropic in memory 0.018, streaming Newton 0.004; COMPAS 0.094, 0.046, 0.013; Adult 1.572, 0.428, 0.261; synthetic 200k rows 5.108, 2.035, 1.193.
  • The grid version is not faster than the in-memory search (the saving is memory). The Newton version is 2-4 times faster than the in-memory search and 4-6 times faster than the same epochs of NumPy minibatch SGD. For a neural network the per-row quantity gᵀ∇f_i is one forward-mode JVP per batch (described, not tested).

Verdict. The earlier wall-clock disadvantage came from the first implementation. The step search is no longer the bottleneck. The SGD baseline here is a NumPy loop; a GPU framework has a different cost profile.

E19 Tilted objective (tilted ERM): tested, then removed

Status: removed from scope; code and results remain in git history

Question. Does a tilted training objective, (1/θ) log mean exp(θ·loss), change the final model beyond reweighting?

What we did. Exact Newton solves for θ in {0.5, 1, 2, 5} against ordinary training, a class-reweighting control matched on training accuracy, and a group-robust control, on 20 splits of four datasets.

E20 Rebalance Guard: worst-case-aware rebalancing with a stability check

Status: completed; research/rebalance_guard (pre-registered)

Question. Does minimizing EV@R, and bagging the estimate, help out of sample for a long-only monthly portfolio?

What we did. Ten US stocks, 304 months (1993-2018), 756-day lookback, 10 bps costs, cap 0.30 per stock (sensitivity: no cap), paired stationary bootstrap. Strategies: equal weight, minimum variance and minimum EV@R, each with a point estimate and a bagged estimate of 30.

Outcome.

4.1 What the adapter is worth

  • Measurement. The tail report, stability_check and per-group statements tell a team how much its decisions depend on the seed, for whom, and which fix it needs. This is useful whichever fix is chosen.
  • For logistic models the exact polish is a cheap, effective fix, and it makes the final model nearly independent of which optimizer ran first.
  • For trees and networks the adapter is seed averaging with calibration, which is equivalent to spending the same compute on more trees. The adapter wraps the model you already have and picks the mode.
  • The risk-averse step is a self-tuning short-step rule with a harm-control knob (θ). It is not a separate source of stability, group improvement or fewer regressions on these data.

4.2 Suggested next steps

  1. Experiment C: large datasets with only a few forced passes (Adult at 5k, 20k and full size, 0.25 to 2 passes). It is the one regime where choosing a short step carefully could still matter, and the streaming step search makes it practical.
  2. Gate 0b: retrain the exactly solved model on bootstrap resamples and on a time-ordered next-period split, to see whether data-driven instability exists even with a perfect optimizer.
  3. A real neural network and an LLM run: use the JVP route for the per-row directional derivative, and the LLM harness once model access is available.
  4. Update the content (tutorial, scripts, video) to describe the risk-averse step as above; the tutorial and fact sheet already carry the main correction.

4.3 Limits that apply to everything above

  • Mostly one small, old dataset (German Credit, 1990s); other datasets only for some experiments. Results describe training behaviour, not current lending.
  • Mostly logistic regression and the model families tested on German Credit; deep networks and LLMs untested.
  • Intervals reflect training randomness (and, where stated, resampling of splits), not the sampling of applicants. Groups are small (36 to 466 cases).
  • Fixing training randomness makes decisions reproducible; it does not fix what the model learned. The exactly trained model still makes many wrong decisions for some groups (for example 24.7% of repaying "housing for free" applicants refused).
  • Sex, age and foreign-worker status were never model inputs and are used only to describe where wrong decisions occur; nothing here is a finding of bias.
